*From settling on a game idea, keeping the project small and choosing an engine, to laying out your game, design document and low-fidelity prototype visually and collecting feedback, and finally setting up GitHub version control and moving development forward with placeholder assets. A useful reference for anyone about to start their first game.*

Making a whole video game is an enormously difficult task, whether you work alone or with a team. There is a lot involved, and it is easy to overlook some crucial early steps that can decide how well your game turns out later on.

You may know that I recently stopped a game I had been working on for almost a year. Many of the problems late in development came from my serious lack of preparation. But as I start my new game, I'll do everything I can to plan it well and keep it as organized as possible.

So come along and start making my game with me! I'll teach you what you should do when you begin game development, and I'll try to make it interactive and fun, because I know a lot of this stuff is easy to ignore.

Strictly speaking, none of these steps is required to start making a game, but I strongly recommend them, and most of them are actually industry standards in development and design. So I hope you'll learn something new from this video.

Without further ado, let's get started!

First, we need to settle on the idea, which is easy. To make a game you need to know what to make, and it all starts with a mechanic, a story or some other idea. I figure that if you want to make a game you probably already have an idea, so I won't spend much time on this part. But if you don't have one yet, or you want a fresh one, I've found two great places for brainstorming: in the shower and outdoors. Honestly, any activity that gets me on my feet or moving always sets my thoughts flowing.

Either way, once you have an idea for a cute game, stop and look at it carefully: how big is this game? How long do you think it will take to make? Multiply that number by 40.

But most importantly, is it a grand, lifetime masterpiece? Preferably not. If this is one of your first few games, make sure the whole idea fits on a single sticky note: every feature, mechanic and story beat, everything.

If you insist on making something bigger, first build the demo using the "sticky note method", and once the demo is completely finished, move up to a bigger game using index cards, but no further than that. Trust me, this will make your game much smaller and you'll be able to finish it relatively quickly.

Another place where a lot of beginners get stuck is the game engine. My sincere advice is this: the sooner you pick one, the sooner you can start making your game. So just pick one; almost every engine can do almost everything. You can do some research, but in the end the engine doesn't matter much, unless you're making a cinematic masterpiece, in which case you can use Unreal 5.

Next, we move on to the next part of making a game: prototyping and the initial coding stage.

At this stage, what we want to confirm is whether the idea is good.

To do that you can make a prototype, which is basically a demo built with placeholder assets and awful art, to see whether the mechanics themselves are fun. That way you avoid wasting time on art, or planning a game that might not be fun at all.

For my game, since I plan to make a top-down dungeon crawler, the final prototype will have multiple layers of enemies, and I'll also show the combat mechanics I have in mind.

But there's another kind of prototype that is much easier to make. Prototypes come in two kinds: low-fidelity and high-fidelity.

For my last game I went straight to high-fidelity, finishing the whole game's features before collecting feedback. That was a big problem, because I had already spent weeks or even months changing things, which creates a huge amount of extra work and is extremely time-consuming.

A very simple way to show your idea and get extremely harsh feedback is to make a low-fidelity prototype. This is a terribly drawn version of your gameplay. It can be a sketch on paper that draws out the concept and explains it, or a short animation that shows only the essence of the game. Since it already looks bad, people are unlikely to go easy on you, because you basically put almost no effort into making it, and that's exactly what we want.

Let me show you an example. In my game, I really want to add rhythm elements. The main character is a dog with mechanical implants, and hitting the beat makes the implants work better. I want to give some reward to "on-beat attacks", so I think adding rhythm-game elements would be super fun.

For example, an attack that misses the beat is a normal attack, just like in a regular top-down game; depending on the attack pattern, if you hit the beat you get a bonus, add something to the combo, or trigger a special effect.

I'm still figuring this out, but since I've shown you a horribly made prototype, you don't need to feel shy about calling it ugly. So if you have any ideas about the rhythm mechanic, feel free to tell me, but I won't just show you. To practice what I preach and lead by example, I actually went outside and asked for feedback on my prototype from the pickiest people I know: my friends. And I really did bring all my notes here, with all the feedback I received written down. Yes, I don't just draw like a 5-year-old, my handwriting is every bit as good.

In the prototype stage, honestly, the more people the better, because you get to see your idea from different angles, which either helps you spot pitfalls or makes it better. So you see, making a prototype and getting feedback is a super important part of the design process, and you should definitely consider doing it for your game.

Now that you finally have a solid plan for the mechanics themselves, it's time to start writing code. Since we made the design document and showed everyone the terrible prototype, we know exactly what we want now, so this should be pretty simple.

We add a jump here, and then... oh, I broke it. It would be great if there were a way to save the state before the code change and track changes to the game more effectively.

OK, let's talk about version control. This is also very important, and when making games, version control can save you in many situations. Version control basically helps you save your coding progress, and you can switch back and forth between the saves to view earlier versions of your game, or simply use it as a "lifeline" to get out of whatever disaster you're stuck in. It's also great for tracking progress: if you feel like you haven't accomplished anything, looking at the version history can show you everything you've already done to get where you are today.

Now you might ask, how do you set up this magical feature? Luckily, there are ready-made apps you can use. Don't worry, the tool we'll use to track saves is GitHub. GitHub is basically a website where lots of people put their code to keep it in code repositories. You can think of a repository as the place where all your saves are kept, which is great because it shows the full history of all your changes. If you have a team, everyone can set it up and work on the project together very easily. We'll set it up in the simplest way:

1. Sign up for an account: first you need to sign up for a GitHub account, right on their website.
2. Download the software: next, download GitHub Desktop, also from their website. After you open GitHub Desktop, it looks like this.
3. Prepare the game: before we start, let's open Godot and build the game itself (if you already have a game, you can skip this part).
4. Sign in to the software: once the game is built, we go back to GitHub Desktop and click the option in the top left, where you sign in to GitHub. It will give you an external link to sign in through your browser, so just sign in there.
5. Add the project: after signing in, we click "Add an Existing Repository from your local drive", find your game folder here and select it as the repository. If you can't find the folder, when you open the Godot app it lists these folders by name.
6. Create the repository: after selecting the folder, click "Add Repository". It will tell you this isn't a Git repository, which is true, so click the "create a repository" link and it takes you here. These are all the options for a repository. Make sure the repository's name matches your game's name, so you don't have to move a bunch of files later.

As for the license, in most cases you don't really need one, but you can always add it later.

The ".gitignore" file has an option for Godot, which is great, and they also provide options for other engines (like Unity and Unreal). This file basically stops unnecessary files from being saved to the repository.

7. Publish and commit: after creating the repository, click the "Publish repository" option. Now you can go to GitHub and view the repository itself, which is basically where you look at all the changes made over time.

Let's actually make a change so you can see what I mean. I just added an image in Godot and put it on the screen. To save the level, I can go back to GitHub Desktop and now I can see the change.

In the bottom left you can commit these changes, which basically creates a save. I'll call this save "added dog" and write a short description of what I did. Once that's done, I can click commit to the main branch, and it will save a save point on the computer, but it hasn't been uploaded to GitHub yet. To upload it to GitHub, we just need to push the changes. Luckily, GitHub Desktop will prompt you on how to do it. You can follow the on-screen instructions, but after a few commits it basically becomes muscle memory.

Now our commit is on GitHub, and we can see the history of all changes, which will get a bit richer after a while.

I can actually show you an example, because Godot is on GitHub, and it has 77,000 commits, which is crazy! It's an open-source project, meaning anyone can see the code and help update it, but it's still amazing.

Anyway, that's how you set up version control. Try to get into the habit of committing every time you make a change, and you'll have a great record of your game's update history.

Version control is very important, and I sincerely hope you'll consider using it. I only used it a little on my last game, and I fell into the trap of "only committing big updates". From experience, I recommend you don't do that: commit every small change you can, especially when working with others. Commits should stay small, because saving the state of a huge update can break a pile of other things, and you also don't want to write your commit messages like Shakespearean epics.

But now that version control is set up, we can actually start writing code, so I won't go deep into programming tutorials now; I'll probably put deeper content on my second channel later. But I want to share some general guidelines that can help you with your first game:

First, this initial version is best made entirely of placeholder assets. For my top-down game specifically, there's a lot of art, and some sprite sheets will have hundreds of frames, so before I release the demo I'll use a bunch of placeholder assets. Only when I can't find a placeholder for some mechanic or feature will I draw the art myself, and even then I'll deliberately make the art bad, so I don't spend hours agonizing over how ugly it is.

Then when it's finally time to release the demo, we have two options: either release it with placeholder assets, as long as they're free to use, or use copyrighted assets like I did, which would probably get me a life sentence in Delfino Plaza. To solve this, what I'll do is crudely make all the art myself and then release that "disaster version". But that's fine, you can think of it this way: if people can accept terrible art, they'll definitely like friendly art even more, right?

The most time-consuming thing in my last game was the art, and I think using placeholder assets can speed things up enormously. Yes, my perfectionism breaks me inside when things aren't what I want them to be, but I think this is a big pit that people easily fall into, myself included, and I want to avoid it at all costs.

Also, code organization is crucial. I reviewed my last game on my second channel, and I couldn't even find where the core mechanic starts and ends. I always used "nobody will look at my code" as an excuse, without realizing that "future me" will exist too, so try to keep things organized.

There are some tutorials out there on state machines, containerization and so on. Honestly, these terms sound scary, but they're really just ways of organizing code, and once you learn the basics you won't forget them. I haven't tried this one yet, but I've heard it gets especially good reviews, and a lot of people have recommended it to me.
